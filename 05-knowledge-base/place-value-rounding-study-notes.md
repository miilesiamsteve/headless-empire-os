## 1. Understand place value first

- A digit's value depends on its position in the number. In 3,482.56, the 3 means 3,000, the 4 means 400, the 8 means 80, the 2 means 2, the 5 means five tenths, and the 6 means six hundredths.
- Each place to the left is ten times larger, and each place to the right is ten times smaller.
- The decimal point separates whole-number places from fractional places. Immediately to its right are tenths, hundredths, and thousandths.
- Zeros can hold places even when they are not significant digits. In 0.0042, the zeros show that the 4 is in the thousandths place.
- Your first job is to identify what a question is asking: an exact calculation, a power, a root, or an approximate rounded answer.

## 2. Exercise 1: powers, roots, and types of numbers

- Exercise 1 is on pages 1–2, with its answers on page 8.

### Squares and cubes

- A power tells you how many times to multiply the base by itself. For example, 4³ means 4 × 4 × 4 = 64, not 4 × 3.
- Squaring means using a power of 2. The worksheet's 14² is 14 × 14 = 196.
- Cubing means using a power of 3. The worksheet's (−6)³ is (−6) × (−6) × (−6) = −216.
- A negative number squared becomes positive because a negative multiplied by a negative is positive. Therefore, (−14)² = 196.
- A negative number raised to an odd whole-number power stays negative, while an even power gives a positive result.
- Brackets matter: (−3)² = 9, but −3² means −(3²) = −9. Calculate powers before applying a minus sign outside them.
- For the worksheet expression (−1)² + (−1)³, calculate the two powers first: 1 + (−1) = 0.

### Square roots, cube roots, and equations

- A square root asks which nonnegative number squares to the given number. For example, √25 = 5.
- An equation can have more than one answer: x² = 25 gives x = 5 or x = −5, because both values square to 25. This differs from the single value of √25.
- A negative number has no real square root, because the square of any real number is zero or positive. For this worksheet, √(−64) has no real-number answer.
- Cube roots can be negative. For example, ∛(−125) = −5 because (−5)³ = −125.
- Isolate the power before solving an equation. For x² − 81 = 0, add 81 to both sides to get x² = 81, so x = 9 or −9.
- For x³ + 1,000 = 0, subtract 1,000 from both sides to get x³ = −1,000, so x = −10.
- For x² + 121 = 0, you get x² = −121, which has no real solution.
- Do not lose negative solutions in higher even powers. The worksheet's x⁶ = 64 has two real solutions, x = 2 and x = −2.

### Number types and challenge methods

- Natural numbers are counting numbers such as 1, 2, and 3. Some courses include zero, but negative numbers are not natural numbers under either convention.
- Integers are whole numbers including negative numbers and zero, such as −9, 0, and 99.
- Rational numbers can be written as a fraction of integers with a nonzero denominator. Every integer is rational; for example, −999 = −999/1.
- A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 with exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself.
- Prime factorisation writes a number as a product of primes. For example, 64 = 2⁶ = 8² = 4³, so it is both a square and a cube. Similarly, 729 = 3⁶ = 27² = 9³.
- To complete a value table, substitute each given x into the expression separately. For example, at x = −2, x² + x = 4 − 2 = 2.
- To test a proposed solution, substitute it into the original equation. In x³ − x = 120, x = 5 works because 125 − 5 = 120; x = −5 does not because −125 + 5 = −120.
- For challenge questions, explain why the answer works rather than giving only a number.

## 3. Exercise 2: index laws

- Exercise 2 is on pages 2–3, with its answers on page 8. An index or exponent is the small raised number in a power.
- When multiplying powers with the same base, add their exponents: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ. For example, 3² × 3 = 3² × 3¹ = 3³.
- This works because you are counting repeated factors: (3 × 3) × 3 contains three factors of 3.
- When dividing powers with the same nonzero base, subtract the exponents: aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ. For example, 8³ ÷ 8 = 8².
- When raising a power to another power, multiply the exponents: (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ. For example, (5³)² = 5⁶.
- A nonzero number to the power 0 equals 1. For example, 15³ ÷ 15³ = 15⁰ = 1.
- A negative exponent means a reciprocal, not a negative answer. For example, 3⁻² = 1/3² = 1/9.
- The base must match before you add or subtract exponents. For example, 4⁵ ÷ 2³ becomes (2²)⁵ ÷ 2³ = 2¹⁰ ÷ 2³ = 2⁷.
- You can use a known power to rewrite another expression. If 5⁴ = 625, then 625² = (5⁴)² = 5⁸.
- To find a missing exponent, write an equation for the exponents. If 4² × 4ⁿ = 4⁵, then 2 + n = 5, so n = 3.
- The multiplication rule does not apply to addition. For example, 2² + 2³ = 4 + 8 = 12, not 2⁵.
- A pattern for one base may not work for another. The worksheet compares sums of powers: 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 = 15 = 2⁴ − 1, but 1 + 3 + 9 + 27 = 40, which is not 3⁴ − 1 = 80.
- Swapping the base and exponent usually changes the answer. Although 2⁴ = 4² = 16, it does not follow that 3⁴ = 4³; these equal 81 and 64.
- When a question asks for index form, leave the answer as a power, such as 2⁷. When it asks for its numerical value, calculate 128.

## 4. Exercise 3: multiplying and dividing by 0.1 and 0.01

- Exercise 3 is on pages 4–5, with its answers on page 9.
- Because 0.1 is one tenth, multiplying by 0.1 is the same as dividing by 10. For example, 60 × 0.1 = 6.
- Because 0.01 is one hundredth, multiplying by 0.01 is the same as dividing by 100. For example, 600 × 0.01 = 6.
- Dividing by 0.1 is the same as multiplying by 10. For example, 6 ÷ 0.1 = 60, because 60 tenths fit into 6.
- Dividing by 0.01 is the same as multiplying by 100. For example, 6 ÷ 0.01 = 600, because 600 hundredths fit into 6.
- In place-value terms, × 0.1 makes every digit's value ten times smaller, while ÷ 0.1 makes it ten times larger.
- For 8.7 × 0.1, divide 8.7 by 10 to get 0.87. For 5.6 ÷ 0.01, multiply 5.6 by 100 to get 560.
- Multiplying a positive number by 0.1 or 0.01 makes it smaller. Dividing a positive number by either makes it larger; division does not always make a number smaller.
- Check with the inverse operation. If 27.9 × 0.01 = 0.279, then 0.279 ÷ 0.01 should return 27.9.
- For missing-operation questions, decide whether the answer needs to be larger or smaller before choosing multiplication or division.

### Applying the rules in word problems

- Identify what the letters stand for before using a formula. In the worksheet, E = 0.1A means the extra tile area is one tenth of the wall area.
- The wall is 2 m by 0.6 m, so A = 2 × 0.6 = 1.2 m². Its extra tile area is E = 0.1 × 1.2 = 0.12 m².
- If you needed the total tile area, you would add the wall and extra areas: 1.2 + 0.12 = 1.32 m². The extra area alone is not the total.
- A discount of D = 0.01C is one hundredth of the cost. On a cost of $195, the discount is $1.95.
- Triangle area is A = ½bh. To find b, multiply both sides by 2 and divide by h, giving b = 2A/h.
- For the worksheet triangle with area 1.16 m² and height 0.1 m, b = 2 × 1.16 ÷ 0.1 = 23.2 m.
- Keep units correct: length is measured in metres, while area is measured in square metres.

## 5. Exercise 4: rounding to significant figures

- Exercise 4 is on pages 6–7, with its answers on page 10. This is the main rounding section.
- Rounding replaces a number with a nearby, less precise number that is easier to use. The symbol ≈ means approximately equal to.
- Significant figures, abbreviated s.f., are counted from the first nonzero digit on the left.
- To round, locate the last digit you must keep and look at the single digit immediately after it.
- If that next digit is 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4, keep the last retained digit unchanged. If it is 5, 6, 7, 8, or 9, increase the last retained digit by 1.
- Replace discarded whole-number digits with zeros so the size of the number stays correct. Discard unwanted decimal digits, keeping any zeros needed to show the requested precision.

### Whole-number examples

- The worksheet rounds 35,680 to 1 s.f. Keep the 3 and inspect the next digit, 5; increase 3 to 4 and replace the remaining places with zeros, giving 40,000.
- To round 4,823 to 2 s.f., keep 4 and 8. The next digit is 2, so the answer is 4,800.
- To round 2,374,650 to 3 s.f., keep 2, 3, and 7. The next digit is 4, so the answer is 2,370,000, not 237.
- Rounding can carry into a new place. For example, 9.98 to 2 s.f. is 10, which can be written 1.0 × 10¹ to make the two significant figures explicit.

### Decimal examples and zeros

- In 0.002432, the first significant digit is 2; the zeros before it only locate the decimal places.
- To round 0.002432 to 1 s.f., keep 2 and inspect 4. The result is 0.002.
- To round 0.002432 to 2 s.f., keep 2 and 4 and inspect 3. The result is 0.0024.
- To round 0.00205884 to 3 s.f., count 2, 0, and 5 as the first three significant digits. The next digit is 8, so the result is 0.00206.
- Zeros between nonzero digits count as significant: the zero in 2.05 counts.
- Trailing decimal zeros can show precision. The worksheet's 0.03963 to 2 s.f. is 0.040; the final zero shows the second significant figure.
- Similarly, 0.1954 to 2 s.f. is 0.20, and 19.985 to 3 s.f. is 20.0. Do not remove the final zero when it is needed to show the requested precision.

### Significant figures versus decimal places

- Decimal places, abbreviated d.p., count every digit after the decimal point. Significant figures start at the first nonzero digit, wherever it is.
- For example, 0.00205884 to 3 d.p. is 0.002, but to 3 s.f. it is 0.00206.
- Always underline or note whether the question asks for s.f. or d.p. before calculating.

### Rounding calculations and checking accuracy

- Keep full calculator accuracy during the calculation and round once at the end unless a question specifically asks for an estimate.
- The worksheet's two journey distances total 1,275.3 + 1,445.2 = 2,720.5 km. Rounded to 2 s.f., the answer is 2,700 km.
- If asked to round one original number to several accuracies, start from the original each time. Repeatedly rounding a rounded answer can produce errors.
- For the worksheet number 530.403977, the answers to 1, 2, and 3 s.f. are 500, 530, and 530. The last two look identical in ordinary notation; 5.3 × 10² and 5.30 × 10² make their different precision clear.
- The same number to 4, 5, and 6 s.f. is 530.4, 530.40, and 530.404, respectively.
- Check that the rounded number is close to the original. Turning a number in the millions into 237 cannot be a sensible rounding result.
